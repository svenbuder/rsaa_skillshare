
# Skillshare: Networking & Collaboration in Astronomy
## Building the collaborations that shape your career

Sven Buder

Based on collected advice from RSAA faculty and postdocs.

> “Your colleagues of tomorrow are in your meetings today.”

---

# Why Networking Matters

## Modern astronomy is collaborative

- Surveys
- Instruments
- Software
- Ideas
- Careers

> Most opportunities come through people who know your work.

---

# Reframing Networking

## Networking is NOT:
- collecting business cards
- talking to famous people only

## Networking IS:
- finding shared interests
- helping each other & building trust
- scientific curiosity

> “Scientists love to know, but love even more to talk about their science.”

---

# Collaboration Map

## Careers are shaped through people

- peers (aka former students)
- surveys
- unexpected collaborations
- interdisciplinary links

---

# Unexpected Paths

## Smaller collaborations are often not planned

- conference coffee chats
- asking a question after a talk
- software help becoming a paper
- side discussions becoming projects

> Small interactions can have long-term consequences.

---

# Advice from Scientists

- “Do things because you are curious.”
- “Things always take longer than expected.”
- “Network with students and ECRs.”
- “Do not take your results personally.”
- “Build genuine human relationships.”

---

# What Can You Offer?

## Networking is not only about what you gain

### Skills
coding help, observing help, data analysis

### Ideas
questions, perspectives, curiosity

### Reliability
follow-through, communication, support

---

# Before a Conference

## Before the conference:
- look at the program
- identify people/projects and read their latest papers
- prepare 2–3 questions
- optional: contact people early so they have seen your name

> Conferences are easier when you have a conversation starter.

---

# During a Conference

## Good networking moments:
- after talks
- poster sessions
- coffee breaks
- conference dinners
- walks between sessions

> You do not need many conversations. You need meaningful ones.

---

# Starting Conversations

## Easy conversation starters

- “I enjoyed your talk/slides/animation/metaphor because…”
- “How did you get into this project?”
- “What are you excited about right now?”
- “How is this problem evolving?”
- “I am doing research in ... Is there anything that I could contribute to?”

---

# Listening Matters

> “Let them do the talking.”

- ask follow-up questions
- be genuinely curious
- people remember good listeners
- senior people will often share exciting ideas (that they may not work on)

---

# Following Up

## After the conference:
- short email/message
- paper link
- code/resource
- proof-of-concept plot for a science idea
- future coffee/chat

> Most collaborations grow slowly.

---

# Pitch Exercise

## Goal: Know your audience

Your pitch needs to change depending who you talk to:
- an expert in your field
- an astronomer in a different field
- a non-astronomer (who care more about ...  than astronomy)
- an funding agency/industry partner/public audience

---

# Bad vs Good Pitch

## Bad
- assuming they already know it all
- jargon
- too many details
- no clear message
- you do not try to connect with them on a personal level

## Good
- enthusiasm
- clear problem
- why it matters to them (e.g. a method that may also work for them)
- why it matters 

---

# Exercise Time: Pitch to a specific audience

## 3 minutes preparation
## 2 minutes each
## Listener gives feedback

> Listener: Take a moment to imagine being ...
> Pitcher: Focus on curiosity and connecting to your listener, not perfection.

# Your Peers Matter

## Your future collaborators are often:
- current students
- postdocs
- workshop colleagues
- your supervisor's network

> People grow together scientifically.

---

# Reliability Matters

## People remember:
- reliability
- kindness
- good communication
- follow-through

More than:
- perfect brilliance

---

# Networking works best when you:
- are genuinely curious
- help others
- stay in touch
- think long-term

> Good science is built by communities.
